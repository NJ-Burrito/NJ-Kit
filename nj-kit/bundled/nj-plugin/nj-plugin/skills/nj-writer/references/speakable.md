# Speakable: writing for the ear

The listener has no spelling, no second pass and no scroll-back. A sentence
that is good on the page can be unspeakable — and the only test that settles
it is saying it aloud, on a stopwatch, in the register the film asks for.

## The four gates

Run every line. Each is free and takes seconds.

### 1. Breath
Say it aloud in the target register without rushing. If you run out of air, or
if it needs a comma to survive, cut it in two. One idea per line.
- Ceiling: ~20 words at 140 wpm is 8.6 s — longer than most beats and longer
  than one comfortable breath.
- **Tell:** you find yourself speeding up to reach the end. That is the line
  telling you it is too long, not you reading badly.

### 2. Tongue
- Repeated sibilants across a word boundary ("this session starts", "tests
  stress").
- Consonant clusters: `-sts`, `-cts`, `-sks` before another consonant.
- Long multisyllabic words chosen to impress: they trip the reader and the
  listener processes them late.
- Arabic: clusters of emphatic letters, and a تاء مربوطة followed by a hamza —
  say it; don't reason about it.

### 3. Numbers
- Round unless the exact figure *is* the point: "more than five hundred", not 514.
- Spoken form in the script: "three hundred dollars", not "$300"; spell out
  thousand/million.
- **One number per line.** Two numbers in one sentence is one number the
  listener keeps and one they lose.
- Ambiguous aloud, all forbidden: `1-2` (one to two? one dash two?), version
  strings ("3.1.0"), times without a marker ("5:40" — is it dread or a
  duration?), ranges spoken as "to" next to another number.
- Arabic: Arabic-Indic vs Western numerals never mixed in the same script; say
  which the film uses and keep it.

### 4. Homophones and heard-ambiguity
- Homophones: their/there, its/it's — invisible on the page, heard as either.
- Words that are a different word when heard fast: "a tester" / "attest her",
  "our own" / "aroun'".
- Product names that collide with a common noun in the sentence around them.
- Acronyms: fine if the audience says them daily (QA); spelled out otherwise.

## Two more things the ear notices

**Stress.** Mark the stressed word in each line when you hand the script over
(bold, or a note column). An unmarked line gets a default read, and a default
read puts the stress on the wrong word about a third of the time.

**Subtext.** A first-person line that states its own feeling is on-the-nose and
flattens (McKee). *"I'm frustrated"* is worse than *"I've typed this twice and
deleted it twice."* The second is the feeling; the first is a label on it. In a
30–90 s piece, subtext is also economy: the concrete line does the work of a
stated emotion and a demonstrated fact at once.

## The stopwatch test, verbatim

The cheapest settling test in the archive, and it needs no artwork:

> Read the beat's lines aloud onto a phone against a stopwatch, eyes shut, in
> the target register. If they end at or past the beat's runtime with no gap
> you would call a breath, the beat does not exist yet.

Ten minutes, zero build. Run it before anything expensive is authored
(`research/114`, kill criterion for objection 1).
