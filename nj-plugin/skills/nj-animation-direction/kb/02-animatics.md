## A. What an animatic / story reel is for, and when it is built

**AN-001** An animatic is a mock-up of still images (usually from a storyboard) timed to rough dialogue or a rough soundtrack, to show how picture and sound work together.
Source: Wikipedia, "Animatic", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animatic (retrieved 2026-11-13). Type: secondary. Confidence: paraphrase.

**AN-002** Purpose: animators and directors use animatics to work out screenplay, camera positioning, shot list and timing problems BEFORE committing to full animation, which is costly. Ad agencies use them as cheap test commercials.
Source: Wikipedia, "Animatic", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animatic (2026-11-13). Type: secondary. Confidence: paraphrase.

**AN-003** In Storyboard Pro a new storyboard is already a rough animatic (every panel one second long); the animatic work is retiming those panels. "An animatic is a video that plays out each panel of your storyboard."
Source: Toon Boom, Storyboard Pro 20 docs, "How to Create an Animatic", https://docs.toonboom.com/help/storyboard-pro-20/storyboard/getting-started/animatic.html (2026-11-13). Type: primary (tool docs). Confidence: quote (fetch-extracted) for the definition; paraphrase for the one-second default.

**AN-004** Timing principle for building one: set each panel's length to fit the action, and each scene's length to fit the intended pace of the story.
Source: Toon Boom, same page as AN-003; same wording surfaced in a search summary of the SBP 7 page. Type: primary. Confidence: paraphrase.

**AN-005** At Pixar the animatic is called the story reel. Pete Docter compares it to a first cut, except the team can go back and "reshoot" the scene. Editor Ken Schretzmann (Toy Story 3, Cars): "On live action, you shoot first and edit later. In animation, you edit first and then shoot it later."
Source: No Film School, "Watch: How Do You Actually Edit an Animated Movie?", https://nofilmschool.com/2017/01/what-does-an-animation-editor-do (2026-11-13). Type: secondary (reporting named editors/director). Confidence: quote (fetch-extracted) for Schretzmann; paraphrase for Docter.

**AN-006** Pipeline position: once storyboards are approved they are assembled into a story reel that goes to LAYOUT (backgrounds/environments), then to animators. For the first Incredibles Brad Bird "spelled out the whole film in the story reels", with animatics and camera moves.
Source: Frame.io blog, "Incredibles 2 and an Inside Look at the Secret to Pixar's Success", https://blog.frame.io/2018/06/18/making-incredibles-2/ (2026-11-13). Type: secondary (interviews with Pixar staff). Confidence: quote (fetch-extracted).

**AN-007** The reel stays soft deep into production: shots can be sent back "three or four times before they're satisfied"; a Pixar film is about 75% complete before final editing; Bird: "It's going to be ugly until it's not."
Source: Frame.io, same page as AN-006. Type: secondary. Confidence: quote (fetch-extracted); the 75% figure is single-source.

**AN-008** Disney screening cadence (Jeff Draheim, editor of Moana, Frozen): story reels are shown "About every three or four months over the years-long process"; about 20 key people in a small "sweat box" screening room analyse every shot; late in production sessions go "from once or twice a week to once or twice a day"; for preview audiences "half of it is in the storyboard version".
Source: Cinemontage, "An Animated Discussion of Editing" (April 2017 panel), https://cinemontage.org/?p=11233 (2026-11-13). Type: primary (editor speaking on his own practice; 2017, practice may have changed). Confidence: quote (fetch-extracted).

**AN-009** Scratch audio is made in-house: crew record their own voices, "a scratch track as a living, breathing script to guide us through the process" (Draheim).
Source: Cinemontage, same page as AN-008. Type: primary. Confidence: quote (fetch-extracted).

**AN-010** The reel is one connected thing: "If there's a change in Act I, it'll have a ripple effect in Act III." (Draheim). Implication: re-check pacing downstream after any early change.
Source: Cinemontage, same page as AN-008. Type: primary. Confidence: quote (fetch-extracted); the implication is mine.

**AN-011** Mark Kennedy (Disney story artist) on the reel and cadence: a story reel is "about 90 minutes of storyboards, temp music and temp dialogue"; a sequence gets about a week to board and a week to address notes; studio policy is a full screening every 12-16 weeks; the story artist pitches the finished sequence to the room.
Source: Story Struggles blog, "Mark Kennedy Disney story process" (compiled from Kennedy's Instagram thread, 2021-01-15), https://storystruggles.blogspot.com/2021/01/mark-kennedy-disney-story-process.html (2026-11-13). Type: secondary (a compilation of Kennedy's own posts). Confidence: quote (fetch-extracted); single-source.

**AN-012** Why iterate at board stage: "We re-do our work over and over so other departments won't have to"; re-doing storyboards "is much cheaper than, say, building a digital set and throwing it out".
Source: Story Struggles blog, same page as AN-011. Type: secondary. Confidence: quote (fetch-extracted); single-source.

**AN-013** Blender Open Movie (Mango) practice: edit the storyboards down into a loosely timed animatic first "to try and give us an idea of timing"; goal "by the end of the day we're going to have a film. Not a finished film - but all the shots will be in there"; the author expected to be cutting shots a bit short and planned to compare early timing estimates with final.
Source: Blender Foundation, Mango production blog, "Editing the Storyboard", http://mango.blender.org/production/editing-the-storyboard/ (2026-11-13). Type: primary (production blog). Confidence: quote (fetch-extracted).

**AN-014** An animatic is also a cost/risk tool: it surfaces pacing and composition problems early and gives a clearer sense of how long each scene will take to produce; typical sequence is storyboard first, animatic second, locked before production. A scratch track first is "common" and gives a reference for pacing, interactions and emotional beats.
Source: CG Wire blog, "How Animatics Bring Stories to Life (2026)", https://blog.cg-wire.com/how-animatics-bring-stories-to-life/ (2026-11-13). Type: secondary, vendor. Confidence: paraphrase (the page says the animatic is "locked in its final form" before production; it does not split locks by pass - see Open questions).

## B. Building one: timing, holds, sound, camera

**AN-015** Storyboard Pro timing mechanics: build in Timeline view; type exact duration as H:MM:SS:FF; nudge a panel one frame at a time; dragging a panel edge ripples all later panels by default; Alt-drag changes the neighbour instead; Animatic Editing Mode leaves gaps instead of rippling.
Source: Toon Boom SBP 20 docs (see AN-003). Type: primary. Confidence: paraphrase.

**AN-016** Camera and sound in the animatic: animate camera moves to preview "how the action will read against framing and camera motion"; animate layers inside panels to clarify action and timing; add provisional or final sound effects, voice clips and music so action is timed to them (a draft sound mix); import still images/video for captions, logos, overlays instead of drawing them into panels.
Source: Toon Boom SBP 20 docs (see AN-003). Type: primary. Confidence: paraphrase.

**AN-017** Transitions in the animatic: dissolve, edge wipe, clock wipe, slide. Adjacent scenes overlap during a transition, so lengthening a transition ripples later scenes earlier/later; preview before, during and after each timing change.
Source: Toon Boom SBP 20 docs (see AN-003). Type: primary. Confidence: paraphrase.

**AN-018** Each panel carries four caption types: Dialogue, Action Notes, Slugging, Notes. Slugging captions "are typically added later, while making the storyboard" and carry the timing of dialogue and actions that the animatic relies on.
Source: Toon Boom SBP 20 docs, "How to Add a Script and Captions", https://docs.toonboom.com/help/storyboard-pro-20/storyboard/getting-started/captions.html (2026-11-13). Type: primary. Confidence: quote (fetch-extracted) for the slugging sentence; paraphrase for the rest.

**AN-019** "Timing and pacing are everything when editing an animatic." Editors decide not just in/out points but "the flow of action in between". Advice: "Edit at a slower pace than seems natural", because animated detail tends to speed the cut up.
Source: Frame.io blog, "Edit First, Shoot Later: A Real-World Look at Editing Animation", https://blog.frame.io/2019/07/15/animation-editing/ (2026-11-13). Type: primary (working animation editor writing about own practice, as far as the page shows). Confidence: quote (fetch-extracted).

**AN-020** Padding rules from one working editor: leave "four frames at the head and tail of any shot with dialogue", and "never less than two seconds for an establishing shot".
Source: Frame.io, same page as AN-019. Type: primary (single editor's practice). Confidence: quote (fetch-extracted); single-source.

**AN-021** Slugging non-dialogue action: to get an accurate runtime, "non-dialogue actions are also slugged in"; animation editing "often involves acting out the scenes of the story" (choreographing fights, vocalizing tire squeals) to get timing.
Source: Frame.io, same page as AN-019. Type: primary. Confidence: quote (fetch-extracted).

**AN-022** Keep the animatic simple: keep dialogue on screen and save L cuts, overlaps, dissolves and push-ins for post, because building them early can leave you short of frames. Also: "Rely on SFX and music as much as visuals for story and timing."
Source: Frame.io, same page as AN-019. Type: primary. Confidence: quote (fetch-extracted); the dissolves/push-ins advice is single-source and conflicts mildly with AN-016 (see Open questions).

**AN-023** Version and hold hygiene: animation is done on 1s, 2s or 3s (a drawing held 1, 2 or 3 frames); plan at least 5% extra fully animated footage beyond the intended run time; "very rarely are the shots ... the first pass"; keep every version of a shot on the timeline until finalised; roughly 2000 JPEGs per ten minutes of content.
Source: Frame.io, same page as AN-019. Type: primary. Confidence: paraphrase with figures single-source.

**AN-024** Starting-point hold guide (the page itself says treat every number as a draft): establishing 3-6 s; standard dialogue 2-4 s; quick reaction 0.5-1.5 s; action cut 0.5-2 s; emotional beat 4-7 s; reveal/discovery 3-5 s; default still about 3 s. Method: read every line aloud with a stopwatch to get real shot length; "hold suspense beats longer and speed up comedy".
Source: Moonb, "How to Make an Animatic: A Producer's Guide", https://www.moonb.io/blog/how-to-make-an-animatic (2026-11-13). Type: secondary, vendor. Confidence: single-source (vendor) - numbers are heuristics, not an industry standard.

**AN-025** Shot list fields and assembly conventions: shot number, shot size, camera angle, one-line action/dialogue note, rough duration; one frame per shot minimum, plus a start and an end frame for a big movement (crossing a room, push-in); name files `Sc01_Sh01` so they sort; scratch dialogue on its own track aligned to panels, adjust shot lengths to fit reactions; export 1920x1080, project fps (24 or 29.97), H.264 MP4.
Source: Moonb, same page as AN-024. Type: secondary, vendor. Confidence: paraphrase, single-source.

**AN-026** Beat-based boarding for ads: a beat is "one job the ad has to do"; a beat usually gets one shot; six fields per frame (shot number + timecode, framing, action, spoken line, on-screen text, sound/transition); split the script …"six to eight shots".
Source: Novo Ads, "Video ad storyboard", https://novoads.ai/blog/video-ad-storyboard (2026-11-13). Type: secondary, vendor. Confidence: paraphrase with short quotes; single-source.

**AN-027** Exposure sheet (X-sheet) is the original frame-accurate timing form: each horizontal line is one frame (1/24 s); the columns are action notes, dialogue (broken into phonetic components in the frame where each occurs), one column per level/layer (drawing numbers in shooting order), background, and a camera column (pans, trucking, field size); a header holds sequence, page, scene number and scene name.
Source: Wikipedia, "Exposure sheet", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exposure_sheet (2026-11-13). Type: secondary. Confidence: paraphrase.

**AN-028** Frame-accurate dialogue breakdown workflow: import the soundtrack, note the scene's start/end timecodes, find the first sound, convert its timecode to a sheet frame, mark each major sound change, then check every frame number before moving on. The page's worked example uses 30 fps, not 24, so the frame rate is a project setting, not a constant.
Source: Brian Lemay, "14 Steps To Successful Lip Sync Breakdown", https://brianlemay.com/Pages/animationschool/animation/lipsyncbook/breaking.html (2026-11-13). Type: secondary (animator's course notes). Confidence: paraphrase.

**AN-029** Reading time for on-screen text is a hard number somewhere: Netflix's English timed-text style guide caps adult programmes at "Up to 20 characters per second", children's at 17, 42 characters per line, at most two lines. Use as an upper bound for caption/title hold time.
Source: Netflix Partner Help Center, "English Timed Text Style Guide", https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/217350977-English-Timed-Text-Style-Guide (2026-11-13). Type: primary (a broadcaster's own spec; it governs subtitles, not on-screen titles). Confidence: quote (fetch-extracted). Applying it to animated title text is my inference.

**AN-030** J-cut: the next shot's audio starts before its picture; L-cut: the previous audio continues over the next picture. Used to give dialogue "a more natural flow", to show a listener's reaction while hearing the speaker, and to open on sound over black.
Source: StudioBinder, "What is a J-cut", https://www.studiobinder.com/blog/what-is-a-j-cut-in-film/ (2026-11-13). Type: secondary. Confidence: quote (fetch-extracted) for the J-cut definition.

## C. Cutting philosophy: Murch's Rule of Six

**AN-031** Murch's six criteria for a cut, in priority order, with his (self-described "slightly tongue-in-cheek") weights: Emotion 51%, Story 23%, Rhythm 10%, Eye-trace 7%, Two-dimensional plane of screen 5%, Three-dimensional space 4%. Murch does not think all six can or should be applied at once.
Source: Video and Filmmaker, "Walter Murch and the 'Rule of Six'", https://videoandfilmmaker.com/wp/?p=3459 (2026-11-13); corroborated by PremiumBeat, https://www.premiumbeat.com/blog/when-and-where-to-make-the-cut-inspired-by-walter-murchs-in-the-blink-of-an-eye/ (2026-11-13). Type: secondary (both summarise "In the Blink of an Eye", Silman-James Press, 1995; the book itself was not opened). Confidence: paraphrase; the percentages are corroborated by two pages that may share one origin.
Note: StudioBinder's article on the same rule gives only "over 50%" for emotion and no other weights (https://www.studiobinder.com/blog/walter-murch-rule-of-six/, 2026-11-13). The shorter list is an omission, not a disagreement.

**AN-032** Priorities when a cut cannot satisfy all six: "If you have to give up something, don't ever give up emotion before story"; emotion is "the thing that you should try to preserve at all costs"; "sacrifice your way up, item by item, from the bottom."
Source: Video and Filmmaker (as AN-031); the first sentence also in StudioBinder (as above). Type: secondary. Confidence: quote (fetch-extracted).

**AN-033** Eye-trace: "My job as an editor is to gently prod the attention of the audience to look at various parts of the frame."
Source: StudioBinder, Walter Murch's Rule of Six, https://www.studiobinder.com/blog/walter-murch-rule-of-six/ (2026-11-13). Type: secondary. Confidence: quote (fetch-extracted).

**AN-034** The blink: Murch noticed Harry Caul blinking close to each cut in The Conversation and concluded people blink when a new whole thought or emotion arrives: "our rate of blinking is somehow geared more to our emotional state and to the nature and frequency of our thoughts." Practical use: cut where a new thought lands, not mid-thought.
Source: PremiumBeat (as AN-031). Type: secondary. Confidence: quote (fetch-extracted).

**AN-035** Dialogue coverage: cutting each full line to its own shot ("Dragnet" style) gives "a shallow simplicity that doesn't reflect the grammar of complex exchanges"; in real conversation we look at the listener while the speaker is still talking, so the question is when to turn; the aim is a cut that feels "surprising yet self-evident at the same time."
Source: PremiumBeat (as AN-031). Type: secondary. Confidence: quote (fetch-extracted).

## D. Shot grammar

**AN-036** 180-degree rule: draw an imaginary axis between two subjects; once established keep every later camera setup on one side to preserve their left/right relationship. For groups, keep setups "in the seats" (audience side of the stage). For moving vehicles/objects keep screen direction consistent across cuts.
Source: StudioBinder, "What is the 180 degree rule", https://www.studiobinder.com/blog/what-is-the-180-degree-rule/ (2026-11-13). Type: secondary. Confidence: paraphrase with one short quote.

**AN-037** Crossing the line disorients and can signal unease; do it "intentionally, and thoughtfully". Ways to reset the line without disorienting: a neutral shot on the line, moving the camera across the line inside one unbroken shot, or a cutaway with no established orientation.
Source: StudioBinder, same page as AN-036. Type: secondary. Confidence: paraphrase.

**AN-038** Screen direction ("the direction that characters and objects move in the scene in relation to the frame"): keep left/right consistent within a scene; do not reverse a walker's direction between shots in the same location; in dialogue, two people talking to each other must not both face the same screen direction.
Source: StudioBinder, "What is screen direction in film", https://www.studiobinder.com/blog/what-is-screen-direction-in-film/ (2026-11-13). Type: secondary. Confidence: quote (fetch-extracted) for the definition; paraphrase for the rules.

**AN-039** Eyeline match: shot 1 shows a character looking off-frame (usually a close-up), shot 2 shows what they look at. Keep shot size, distance, focal length, horizon line and depth of field consistent across the pair; keep the 180-degree rule; match eyelines vertically as well as horizontally (actors of different heights); for groups a two- or three-shot is easier than separate singles.
Source: StudioBinder, "What is an eyeline match", https://www.studiobinder.com/blog/what-is-an-eyeline-match/ (2026-11-13). Type: secondary. Confidence: paraphrase.

**AN-040** Continuity basics: edit on the action ("cutting during a movement rather than between movements"); open a sequence with an establishing (wide) shot, use a re-establishing shot when the scene changes; match on action needs the action framed from at least two positions; shot/reverse-shot alternates roughly opposite directions; 30-degree rule: move the camera at least 30 degrees between two shots of one subject, ideally also change shot size, to avoid a jump cut.
Source: Learn About Film (Tom Barrance), "Continuity: Shoot and Edit so your Movie Makes Sense", https://learnaboutfilm.com/film-language/sequence/ (2026-11-13). Type: secondary. Confidence: quote (fetch-extracted) for "edit on the action"; paraphrase for the rest.

**AN-041** The 30-degree rule is often paired with a focal-length variant (change the lens by at least about 20 mm and the angle by 30 degrees); it exists to avoid jump cuts, which can be spatial or temporal and may be used on purpose for a stylised effect.
Source: web-search result summary pointing at Wikipedia "Jump cut"/"30-degree rule", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/30-degree_rule (page NOT opened). Type: secondary. Confidence: search-snippet. Use only as a lead; AN-040 is the opened source for the 30-degree rule itself.

**AN-042** Shot sizes (StudioBinder grouping): close-ups = ECU, CU, MCU; medium = MS, cowboy, MFS; wide = full, wide, EWS. Angle intents on the page: low angle "Empower", eye level "Connect", high angle "Depower", Dutch "Disorient", overhead "Omniscience", aerial "Awe & Scale". Camera movement list: static, pan, tilt; whip pan, pedestal, truck, zoom, dolly-zoom, aerial; dolly as a mechanism.
Source: StudioBinder, "Camera Shots", https://www.studiobinder.com/blog/types-of-camera-shots-angles-in-film/ (2026-11-13). Type: secondary. Confidence: paraphrase (the fetch returned names and one-word purposes only, not full descriptions).

**AN-043** Basic storyboard vocabulary (Penn State): shot sizes WS, MS, MCU, CU, ECU and extreme wide; eye level is the neutral default, low angle suggests power, high angle weakness; five camera movements - static, pan, tilt, zoom, tracking/"trucking"; when voiceover exists note which part plays over which shot; thumbnails need not be pretty ("break out those stick figures").
Source: Penn State Media Commons, "Storyboarding", https://mediacommons.psu.edu/2013/07/storyboarding (2026-11-13). Type: secondary (university course page). Confidence: paraphrase with one quote.

**AN-044** Shot selection and transitions (Brian Lemay storyboarding notes): "Good shot selection can make a movie very interesting or it can make it very boring"; a new point of view means a new shot or scene; a cross dissolve usually signals a short passage of time, a wipe typically moves to another location without implying time has passed; scenes are active (physical movement) or passive.
Source: Brian Lemay, "Storyboarding Basics", https://brianlemay.com/Pages/animationschool/storyboarding/storyboarding basics.html (2026-11-13). Type: secondary (animation-school notes). Confidence: quote (fetch-extracted) for the first line; paraphrase for the rest.

**AN-045** Motivated camera movement: movement that "the actions on screen dictate"; camera movement should follow the blocking unless it is a character's POV; do not move actors aimlessly just to justify a camera move. Reasons to move: guide attention, reveal gradually for suspense, control pacing, keep fast action from feeling static.
Source: PremiumBeat, "Cinematography Tip: Motivated Camera Movement", https://www.premiumbeat.com/blog/cinematography-tip-motivated-camera-movement/ (2026-11-13). Type: secondary. Confidence: quote (fetch-extracted) for the definition; paraphrase for the rest.

**AN-046** Cinematographer M. David Mullen, ASC: when and why to move the camera matters more than how; justifications are dramatic (heighten tension, move in on an emotional expression) or logical (follow important action); an unnecessary move is often the first thing cut when the first cut runs long.
Source: StudentFilmmakers, "Notes on Camera Movement: When to Move the Camera and Why", https://www.studentfilmmakers.com/notes-on-camera-movement-when-to-move-the-camera-and-why-by-m-david-mullen-asc/ (page returned 403; claims taken from a web-search result summary of it). Type: primary (a working DP on his own practice) but not opened. Confidence: search-snippet.

**AN-047** Staging (Thomas and Johnston, via Wikipedia): "the presentation of any idea so that it is completely and unmistakably clear" - an action, personality, expression or mood; keep focus on what is relevant, avoid unnecessary detail; achieved by character placement, light and shadow, camera angle.
Source: Wikipedia, "Twelve basic principles of animation", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twelve_basic_principles_of_animation (2026-11-13), quoting Johnston and Thomas, *The Illusion of Life* (1981; book not opened). Type: secondary. Confidence: quote (fetch-extracted).

**AN-048** Timing principle (same page): "the number of drawings or frames for a given action, which translates to the speed of the action on film"; correct timing conveys weight, mood, reaction, personality. Anticipation: prepare the audience for an action, including a glance off-screen before someone arrives.
Source: Wikipedia, "Twelve basic principles of animation" (as AN-047). Type: secondary. Confidence: quote (fetch-extracted).

**AN-049** Mark Kennedy's staging tips (as quoted by another blog): only one idea presented at a time, so each new idea gets its own sketch; "The eye will always be drawn to the area of greatest contrast first"; detail pulls the eye to an area; a sketch-level contrast trick may not survive full colour, so insert a close-up first or start close and pull back to the wide; converging perspective lines and varied line weight aid readability.
Source: The Spider Weaver blog, "Storyboarding tips" (quoting Kennedy's Seven Camels blog), https://spiderweaver.blogspot.com/2015/01/storyboarding-tips.html (2026-11-13). Type: secondary (Kennedy's original posts were not reached). Confidence: quote (fetch-extracted); single-source.

**AN-050** Panel-level checks before animating: (1) one clear story beat per panel - if the description needs "and then", split; (2) screen direction consistent; (3) action readable with captions and voiceover hidden - show to someone and ask what happened; (4) decide and …"simplify the action rather than speeding up the delivery".
Source: School of Motion, "5 storyboard checks before you animate", https://schoolofmotion.com/blog/5-storyboard-checks-before-you-animate (2026-11-13). Type: secondary (motion-design school; fits explainer/motion-graphics work directly). Confidence: paraphrase with one quote.

**AN-051** Bruce Block, The Visual Story: contrast = difference, affinity = similarity; contrast raises visual intensity, affinity lowers it; the same principle governs all seven components (space, line, shape, tone, color, movement, rhythm); changing intensity level can support the story's arc. Chapter 9 is "Story & Visual Structures"; the book ties the components to decisions on camera angles, lenses, staging, composition, storyboarding, coverage and editing.
Source: Better Posters review, https://betterposters.blogspot.com/2022/07/review-visual-story.html?m=0 (2026-11-13) and Routledge catalogue page, https://www.routledge.com/The-Visual-Story/Block/p/book/9781138014152 (2026-11-13). Type: secondary (the book's own Chapter 2 text returned 403 at O'Reilly). Confidence: paraphrase; the review states these are its own summaries, not quotes. The intensity-graph/worksheet is NOT confirmed (see Open questions).

## E. Pacing and beat maps

**AN-052** Shot length and pacing: shorter shots read as urgent/fast, longer shots as relaxed/slow; example ASL: The Mist 5.4 s (117 min, 1,292 shots).
Source: Wikipedia, "Average shot length", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Average_shot_length (2026-11-13). Type: secondary. Confidence: paraphrase; the Duration section carries a "no sources" banner, so the pacing claim is uncited there.

**AN-053** 30-second commercial worked breakdown (Apple "Get a Mac", 2006): hook 0:00-0:05, problem 0:06-0:15, solution 0:16-0:25, call to action 0:26-0:30.
Source: Celtx blog, https://blog.celtx.com/?p=13197 (2026-11-13). Type: secondary (a screenwriting-software vendor describing one real ad). Confidence: paraphrase; single worked example, not a standard.

**AN-054** 30-second storyboard table from the beat method: 0:00-0:03 close-up hook; 0:03-0:06 medium problem; 0:06-0:10 insert product; 0:10-0:15 macro demo; 0:15-0:20 medium routine; 0:20-0:25 close-up proof; 0:25-0:30 medium CTA (7 shots; product appears at 0:06).
Source: Novo Ads (as AN-026). Type: secondary, vendor. Confidence: paraphrase; single-source illustrative example.

**AN-055** Five-beat commercial template with timings: hook 0-3 s, problem 3-7, product 7-12, proof 12-24, CTA the last 3-6 s; compressed 15-second version: hook 0-2, problem 2-5, product 5-8, proof 8-12, CTA 12-15. The same source describes the timings as extrapolated, not a published standard.
Source: LlamaGen, "Commercial storyboard template", https://llamagen.ai/blogs/commercial-storyboard-template (web-search result summary; page NOT opened). Type: secondary, vendor. Confidence: search-snippet, single-source.

**AN-056** Explainer voice-over budget: recommended 140-150 words for a 60-second video at about 2.5 words per second (150 wpm); about 240 words per minute is possible but "can sound forced"; "It takes time to hear and process data, so increasing your average speaking rate doesn't help your viewers." For screencast videos the voice-over adapts to the visuals' speed. The page gives no scene count or seconds-per-scene.
Source: Bread & Beyond, https://breadnbeyond.com/how-many-words-does-a-60-seconds-explainer-video-needs/ (2026-11-13). Type: secondary, vendor (explainer studio). Confidence: quote (fetch-extracted) for the processing sentence; paraphrase for numbers. Other vendors in the same search give 130-160 wpm and 150-180 words/min - treat 130-160 wpm as the band.

**AN-057** Runtime check formula: estimated seconds = (word count / words per minute) x 60; verify by reading aloud with a timer. (Example uses 140 wpm.)
Source: Typecast, "Explainer Video Script Template With Voiceover Timing", https://typecast.ai/learn/?p=14931 (web-search result summary; page NOT opened). Type: secondary, vendor. Confidence: search-snippet. The arithmetic is trivial; the citation is only for the explainer-timing practice.

**AN-058** Pacing must vary: critique questions "Is the pacing all the same?", "Are there times where the action [is] fast or slow?", and "Does this variation help tell the story and add interest and excitement?"
Source: Willamette University, IDS 252 3D Animation, "Animatic critique", https://people.willamette.edu/~gorr/classes/ids252Sp14/lectures/animaticCritique.doc (2026-11-13). ## G. Producing an animatic programmatically (data shape and tooling)

**AN-066** OpenTimelineIO is a neutral timeline data model: a Timeline wraps a Stack of Tracks; a Track is an ordered list of Clips, Gaps and Transitions; a Clip has a media reference (target_url, available_range) and a `source_range` that trims it; time is a `RationalTime` (value, rate; e.g. frame 7 at 24 fps) and a range is start_time + duration; a Gap is a transparent placeholder used to pad/offset; a Transition (cross dissolve) takes in/out offsets from its neighbours and does not change track length.
Source: OpenTimelineIO docs, "Timeline structure", https://opentimelineio.readthedocs.io/en/latest/tutorials/otio-timeline-structure.html (2026-11-13). Type: primary (framework docs). Confidence: paraphrase.

**AN-067** Remotion `<Sequence from durationInFrames>`: `from` is the frame at which children assume their own timeline starts; children calling `useCurrentFrame()` see a frame shifted by `from` (at composition frame 30 with `from={30}` the child reads 0); `durationInFrames` defaults to Infinity; layout defaults to absolute-fill, so sequences overlay.
Source: Remotion docs, https://www.remotion.dev/docs/sequence (2026-11-13). Type: primary. Confidence: quote (fetch-extracted) for the quoted phrases; paraphrase otherwise.

**AN-068** Remotion `<Series>`: stitches scenes that play sequentially; each `Series.Sequence` has `durationInFrames`, the next starts after it; only the last may be Infinity; `offset` > 0 inserts a gap, `offset` < 0 overlaps the previous scene (`-10` overlays 10 frames), affecting that sequence and the ones after it.
Source: Remotion docs, https://www.remotion.dev/docs/series (2026-11-13). Type: primary. Confidence: quote (fetch-extracted).

**AN-069** Remotion audio: put the file in `public/` and reference with `staticFile()`; `trimBefore` cuts the start; `durationInFrames` selects the length (at 30 fps `trimBefore={60}` with `durationInFrames={60}` plays 2 s-4 s of the source); drive volume from `useCurrentFrame()` + `interpolate()`.
Source: Remotion docs, https://www.remotion.dev/docs/audio (2026-11-13). Type: primary. Confidence: paraphrase.

**AN-070** HyperFrames: "a free, open-source framework that turns HTML into video"; rendering asks the project for each exact frame instead of depending on live playback, so "a slow machine does not drop moments"; a composition root div carries `data-composition-id`, `data-start`, `data-duration`, `data-width`, `data-height`; clips are `class="clip"` divs; "HTML and CSS control what appears; `data-*` attributes control when it appears" via `data-start`, `data-duration`, `data-track-index`.
Source: HyperFrames docs, https://hyperframes.heygen.com/introduction and https://hyperframes.heygen.com/developers.md (2026-11-13). Type: primary. Confidence: quote (fetch-extracted). Audio placement and determinism rules were NOT found on those pages; see the nj-hyperframes-* skills and the other lanes.

**AN-071** ffmpeg concat demuxer: script file starts `ffconcat version 1.0`; directives `file <path>`, `duration <dur>` (overrides the file's own duration - useful for stills), `inpoint`, `outpoint` (exclusive); with a duration on every file seeking works over the whole output. The "repeat the last image's duration line" caveat for image slideshows was NOT confirmed (page chunk did not include it).
Source: FFmpeg formats documentation, https://ffmpeg.org/ffmpeg-formats.html#concat (2026-11-13). Type: primary (tool docs). Confidence: paraphrase.

**AN-072** ffmpeg `xfade`: "Apply cross fade from one input video stream to another"; `duration` 0-60 s, default 1; `offset` "Set cross fade start relative to first input stream in seconds", default 0; example `xfade=transition=fade:duration=2:offset=5`. Search results add that the offset should not exceed (first clip duration - transition duration) and that output length = sum of inputs minus the transition (search-snippet).
Source: FFmpeg filter docs mirror, https://ayosec.github.io/ffmpeg-filters-docs/8.0/Filters/Video/xfade.html (2026-11-13); length arithmetic from FFmpeg mailing-list search results (not opened). Type: primary (docs mirror). Confidence: quote (fetch-extracted) for the options; search-snippet for the length arithmetic.

**AN-073** ffmpeg `zoompan` (Ken Burns / push-in on a still): options `zoom` (1-10, default 1), `x`, `y`, `d` = duration in output frames per input image (default 90), `s` output size (default hd720), `fps` (default 25). Example: `zoompan=z='min(zoom+0.0015,1.5)':d=700:x='iw/2-(iw/zoom/2)':y='ih/2-(ih/zoom/2)'`. Set `s` and `fps` explicitly to match the project or the stills come out at 720p/25.
Source: FFmpeg filter docs mirror (6.0), https://ayosec.github.io/ffmpeg-filters-docs/6.0/Filters/Video/zoompan.html (2026-11-13). Type: primary (docs mirror; official ffmpeg.org page could not be paged to that section). Confidence: quote (fetch-extracted). The "set s and fps explicitly" advice is my inference from the defaults.

**AN-074** Blender ships a Storyboarding app template (2D Animation template + Video Sequence Editor in Sync Scene mode): each shot is its own scene, the Sequencer is the master timeline where shot order and timing are adjusted; supports drawn boards and 3D-layout "3D animatics"; the Storypencil add-on copies sequencer audio into the source scene and renders scene strips.
Source: Blender manual, Video Editing > Storyboarding, https://docs.blender.org/manual/en/latest/video_editing/storyboarding/index.html (web-search result summary of the manual; the page fetch returned only navigation). Type: primary (tool docs) but not opened. Confidence: search-snippet.

**AN-075** Storyboard Pro exports the finished animatic as a movie file, or as Harmony scenes the same length as the storyboard scenes (including their share of the soundtrack). Via the Flix integration, boards export as PNG and "markers and dialogues" are not exported by default.
Source: Toon Boom SBP 22 export docs, https://docs.toonboom.com/help/storyboard-pro-22/storyboard/getting-started/export.html; Foundry Flix 5.4 docs, https://learn.foundry.com/flix/5.4/Content/User_Guide/Third_Party_Applications/exporting_storyboard_pro.html (web-search result summaries; pages NOT opened). Type: primary (tool docs) but not opened. Confidence: search-snippet.

## Rules for the skill

Each rule is imperative; AN ids in brackets are the evidence. Rules marked (synthesis) combine several sources or add my inference and should be tested before being treated as house law.

### 1. Why and when
1. Build the animatic after the script/beat map and boards exist and before any final animation; treat it as the cheapest place to change pacing, order and clarity. [AN-002, AN-012, AN-014, AN-013]
2. Treat the reel as living: version it, expect repeated rounds, and re-check pacing downstream after any early change. [AN-007, AN-008, AN-010]
3. In animation you "edit first and shoot later": decide shot count, length and order in the animatic, because late recuts are hard. [AN-005]

### 2. Build order and what each pass settles (synthesis)
The sources describe the pieces but not a standard pass list (see Open questions). Use this order and record which pass a note belongs to:
4. Pass 0, beat map: split the script into beats, each with one job and a start/end time; first cut by about 0:03 for ads; read aloud with a stopwatch. [AN-026, AN-056, AN-057]
5. Pass 1, boards: one idea and one story beat per panel; start/end frame for any big movement; captions hidden, does the action still read? [AN-025, AN-049, AN-050]
6. Pass 2, timing: lay scratch VO first, time every panel to it, slug non-dialogue action, then adjust shot lengths to reactions. Edit slower than feels natural. [AN-016, AN-019, AN-021, AN-025]
7. Pass 3, camera and transitions: add only motivated camera moves and plain cuts; leave L-cuts, overlaps and fancy dissolves for the later pass. [AN-016, AN-022, AN-045]
8. Pass 4, sound: temp music and SFX are draft; use SFX and music as much as picture to carry story and timing. [AN-016, AN-022]
9. Pass 5, review and lock: run the checklist below, record one decision, freeze the version number that script, boards, audio and animatic share. [AN-063, AN-064]

### 3. Timing and holds
10. Keep frame rate a project parameter (24, 25, 29.97, 30) and store durations in frames; convert to seconds only for display. [AN-015, AN-027, AN-028, AN-066]
11. Start from these hold budgets and overwrite them by reading aloud: establishing 3-6 s (never under 2 s), dialogue 2-4 s, quick reaction 0.5-1.5 s, action cut 0.5-2 s, emotional beat 4-7 s, reveal 3-5 s, default still ~3 s. They are vendor heuristics, not standards. [AN-020, AN-024]
12. Pad dialogue shots by about four frames at head and tail. [AN-020]
13. Budget narration at 130-160 wpm (about 2.5 words/s); a 60-second explainer carries roughly 140-150 words; if the picture cannot keep up, simplify the action, not the delivery. [AN-056, AN-057, AN-050]
14. Cap on-screen text hold by a reading rate: adult 20 characters/second, 42 characters/line, two lines at most, as an upper bound (subtitle spec applied to titles, my inference). [AN-029]
15. For ads, open fast (first cut by 0:03, opening shots 2-3 s, body 4-5 s, close long enough to read the CTA); a 30-second spot is usually 6-8 shots. [AN-026, AN-054]
16. Vary pace on purpose: fast action, slower emotional or reveal beats; flag any stretch where every shot is the same length. [AN-024, AN-052, AN-058]

### 4. Shot grammar
17. Choose each cut by Murch's order: emotion, then story, then rhythm, then eye-trace, then screen plane, then 3D space; sacrifice from the bottom, never emotion before story. [AN-031, AN-032]
18. Cut where a new thought or emotion lands; avoid line-per-shot mechanical dialogue coverage. [AN-034, AN-035]
19. Cut on action, not between actions; use a wide establishing shot to open a sequence and a re-establish when geography changes. [AN-040]
20. Choose one side of the 180-degree line per scene; keep screen direction and eyelines consistent; cross the line only on purpose, using a neutral shot, an in-shot move or a cutaway. [AN-036, AN-037, AN-038, AN-039]
21. Change both angle and shot size between two shots of one subject (30-degree rule) unless a jump cut is the intended effect. [AN-040, AN-041]
22. Move the camera only when the action, blocking or emotion calls for it; otherwise cut or hold still. [AN-045, AN-046]
23. Stage for clarity: one idea at a time, direct the eye with contrast and detail, remember the eye goes to the area of greatest contrast first. [AN-047, AN-049]
24. Plan intensity across the piece: raise visual contrast (shot size, movement, tone, colour) toward the peak and lower it toward rest. [AN-051] (Graph/worksheet itself unconfirmed.)
25. Write the transition for every adjacent shot pair (cut by default; dissolve = time passes; wipe = place changes). [AN-044, AN-050]
26. Use J-cuts/L-cuts for natural dialogue flow, but add them after the picture-and-timing pass. [AN-022, AN-030]

### 5. Review
27. Review in this order: brief and approver, then story logic and pacing, then continuity, then polish. Separate blocking issues from polish. [AN-063, AN-065]
28. Write each note against a shot id, a version and a timecode, name the problem (not only the fix), tag it story / pacing / visual, and offer an alternative. [AN-060, AN-063, AN-064]
29. Notes identify problems; the director owns the solution. Resolve contradictory notes before sending them on. [AN-059, AN-064]
30. Run the animatic checklist (section below) and record exactly one outcome: approved, changes requested, needs discussion. [AN-061, AN-063]

### 6. Producing it programmatically
31. Keep one data file as the source of truth (shot list + timing sheet); render from it, never hand-edit the video. Shape proposed below. [AN-025, AN-026, AN-027, AN-066]
32. Store timing as integer frames + a project fps (OTIO's RationalTime idea); derive seconds, timecode and `ffmpeg`/Remotion/HyperFrames values from it. [AN-066, AN-067, AN-070]
33. In Remotion map each shot to a `Series.Sequence` with `durationInFrames`; use negative `offset` only for planned overlaps (transitions, L-cuts). Trim audio with `trimBefore`/`durationInFrames`. [AN-067, AN-068, AN-069]
34. In HyperFrames map each shot to a `class="clip"` element with `data-start`, `data-duration`, `data-track-index`; put scratch VO and music on their own tracks. [AN-070]
35. For a no-framework still-based animatic in ffmpeg: concat demuxer with a `duration` per still, `zoompan` for push-ins (set `s`, `fps`, `d` explicitly), `xfade` only where a dissolve is planned, scratch VO muxed on its own track. [AN-071, AN-072, AN-073]
36. Name stills `Sc01_Sh01...` so they sort; export 1920x1080 H.264 at the project frame rate; keep every previous version. [AN-023, AN-025]
37. After every render, run the checklist numerically: total runtime vs target, words per second, caption characters per second, any shot under 2 frames or over its hold budget. [AN-029, AN-056, AN-057]

    "beat": "hook",

The per-frame header fields come from AN-026 (shot number + timecode, framing, action, line, on-screen text, sound/transition); `axis_side`, `screen_dir` and `motivation` serve AN-036/038/045 checks; `start_f`/`dur_f` + fps follow AN-066.

### Animatic checklist before production (synthesis of AN-050, AN-058, AN-061, AN-062, AN-063)
Judge pass/fail per item, with shot id and timecode on every fail.
1. Clarity: with captions and VO hidden, does a stranger say what happened in each shot? [AN-050]
2. One beat per shot; each shot ties to a story beat or production need. [AN-050, AN-063]
3. Geography: screen direction, 180-degree side and eyelines consistent, or the break is intentional. [AN-036-AN-039]
4. Every cut: motivated by emotion/story/rhythm; cut on action; angle and size change enough. [AN-031, AN-040]
5. Every camera move: motivated by action, blocking or emotion. [AN-045]
6. Timing: each shot length feels right; reactions have room; establishing shots not under 2 s; dialogue padded; VO within 130-160 wpm. [AN-020, AN-056, AN-058]
7. Pace varies; no flat stretch; the middle does not sag; opening and ending land. [AN-058, AN-062]
8. Text: within reading-rate caps and not covering key action. [AN-029, AN-063]
9. Sound: scratch VO and temp music mark every emotional beat; nothing needs the final mix to make sense. [AN-009, AN-016]
10. Transitions: every adjacent pair has a decided transition. [AN-050]
11. Total runtime matches target, shot count matches the format (6-8 for a 30-second ad). [AN-026]
12. Version is recorded and shared by script, boards, audio and animatic. [AN-063, AN-064]
