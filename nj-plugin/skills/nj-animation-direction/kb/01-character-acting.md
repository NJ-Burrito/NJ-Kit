## A. Intention, objective, obstacle, thought

**CA-001** Acting is doing: a character that is not doing something to achieve a definable, provable objective is not acting. Write the shot brief as an action, not as a mood.
Source: Ed Hooks, "Disney's 'Frozen': The Acting and Performance Analysis", Cartoon Brew (c. Nov/Dec 2013; page shows only a relative date), https://cartoonbrew.com/ideas-commentary/disneys-frozen-the-acting-and-performance-analysis-97605.html. Retrieved 2026-11-13.
Type: primary (Hooks' own text). Confidence: verified quote ("Acting is doing." / "If a character is not doing anything to achieve a definable and provable objective, he is not acting.").

**CA-002** Hooks' definition of a valid action is: "Action in pursuit of (provable) objective while overcoming an obstacle." So every shot brief needs three slots: objective, action (tactic), obstacle.
Source: same as CA-001. Type: primary. Confidence: verified quote.

**CA-003** Freeze-frame test: stop the shot on any frame and ask the character "what are you doing?"; the answer must be theatrical ("my objective is X; the action I am playing to get it is Y; the obstacle is Z"), not emotional ("I am sad").
Source: same as CA-001. Type: primary. Confidence: paraphrase with the template wording quoted in the article.

**CA-004** "Emotion alone has zero theatrical value" and mood is not acting; emotion is the result of an intention being helped or blocked, not something to animate first. "Emotions tend to lead to actions."
Source: CA-001 article (quote: "The fact is that mood is not acting at all, and emotion alone has zero theatrical value."); and Hooks interview, renderu.com, https://renderu.com/en/articles/post/13139 ("Emotions are not actable."; "Emotions tends to lead to actions."). Type: primary (Hooks' words). Confidence: verified quote.

**CA-005** Hooks' own wording of the thinking-feeling link: "Thinking tends to lead to conclusions, and emotion tends to lead to action." Note: the often-quoted slogan "thought precedes action" is NOT Hooks' verbatim wording in anything I could read; the nearest verified forms are this line and Williams' CA-014.
Source: summary list of Hooks' seven principles, https://www.actingcoachscotland.co.uk/blog/principles-acting-animators-and-actors (author not named on fetch). Type: secondary. Confidence: verified quote of the page, single-source for it being Hooks' exact words.

**CA-006** Play an action until something happens that makes you play a different one; when an obstacle blocks the objective, change tactics rather than the objective. Gives the "beat" rule: a beat = a change of tactic or a new thought.
Source: CA-005 page (quote: "Your character should play an action until something happens to make him play a different action"); also class notes at https://www.thinkinganimation.com/acting-for-animators ("Play an action until something happens that makes you play a different action."). Type: secondary (the second page says its wording is the site author's class notes, not a transcript). Confidence: verified quote of both pages; Hooks attribution is secondary.

**CA-007** "A scene is a negotiation"; a scene is stronger when something is pushed, blocked, avoided, demanded, denied or protected. A single character alone still needs a push against something (a person, an object, themselves).
Source: CA-001 article (quote: "A scene is a negotiation."); thinkinganimation.com/acting-for-animators (class notes, wording theirs). Type: primary for the short quote, secondary for the extension. Confidence: verified quote / paraphrase.

**CA-008** Scenes begin in the middle: the character arrives from somewhere and goes somewhere after; never treat the character as a blank slate at frame 1 of the shot. Write one line of "what just happened" and "what happens next" in the brief.
Source: J.K. Riki, "Acting Tips from Ed Hooks", Animator Island, 2015-05-11 (updated 2016-06-20), https://www.animatorisland.com/?p=3574; same principle listed on the actingcoachscotland page (CA-005). Type: secondary. Confidence: verified quote ("Don't think of them as a blank slate at the start of a shot.").

**CA-009** "Animate the thought": capture at least a reflective shadow of the character's plan. The shot should let the viewer see the character thinking, not only moving.
Source: CA-001 article (quote: "animate the thought," "at least a reflective shadow" of a character's plan). Type: primary. Confidence: verified quote.

**CA-010** Objectives must be provable and concrete: "being happy" is weak; going to a specific place for a stated reason is playable.
Source: Animator Island (CA-008). Type: secondary (summary of Hooks). Confidence: paraphrase, single-source.

**CA-011** Conflict drives emotion; empathy comes from direct action, not from the character announcing a feeling. Avoid idle time (standing or wandering with no objective).
Source: Postcard from GDC 2004: Acting for Animators (report of Hooks' talk), https://www.gamedeveloper.com/marketing/postcard-from-gdc-2004-acting-for-animators (gamesetwatch.com URL redirects here). Type: secondary (reporter's summary). Confidence: verified quote ("it is conflict that drives emotion"; "it is his subsequent action that will convey that emotion").

**CA-012** Make the acting choices that get you into the most trouble; show, don't tell; actions are dominant over words.
Source: CA-001 article (quotes: "In general, it is best to make acting choices that get you into the most trouble."; "Show; don't tell."; "Actions are dominant over words."). Type: primary. Confidence: verified quote.

**CA-013** A strong frame for the whole performance: Hooks says the animator's relationship to the character is "empathetic direction", and animation must give characters "a thinking, feeling brain."
Source: Hooks interview, https://renderu.com/en/articles/post/13139. Type: primary (interview). Confidence: verified quote.

**CA-014** Williams: "The mind is the pilot. We think of things before the body does them. There's always a split second of thinking time before the character …" Put a visible thinking beat before a decision or reaction.
Source: Richard Williams, *The Animator's Survival Kit*, section "Change of Expression" (OCR, caps: "AGAIN, THE MIND IS THE PILOT. WE THINK OF THINGS BEFORE THE BODY DOES THEM. THERE'S ALWAYS A SPLIT SECOND OF 'THINKING TIME' BEFORE THE …"). SK URL above. Type: primary. Confidence: verified quote (OCR). No frame count is given for the "split second".

**CA-015** Williams on anticipation as thinking: "for almost every action we make there is an anticipation. We think of things first, then do them", except pre-programmed responses (shifting gears, getting dressed). Speech works the same way: the brain fixes the sense of what to say, then selects the muscles.
Source: SK, section "Anticipation" (OCR). Type: primary. Confidence: verified quote (OCR).

**CA-016** The thought is shown by a change of expression: Eric Larson's principle (via IL and Williams): a face that lowers its brows into a frown, pauses, then lifts one brow and glances aside reads instantly as a change from one thought to another. Thomas and Johnston: "It is the change of shape that shows the character is thinking. It is the thinking that gives the illusion of life."
Source: IL, "Expressions"/emotions chapter, quotes at the passages "Through a change of expression the thought process was shown" and rule 6 of the expression rules; Williams repeats the Larson anecdote in SK "Change of Expression". Type: primary. Confidence: verified quote.

**CA-017** One key expression per thought: the expression illustrates the thought, not the words, so it stays constant however many words are spoken; it may change in intensity but not in feeling. When a new thought or realization arrives, change to a new key expression; the speed of the change reflects how the character thinks (a sudden realization = a quick change).
Source: IL, section "Listen to Your Track"/dialogue staging. Type: primary. Confidence: verified quote ("The expression chosen is illustrating the thoughts of the character and not the words he is saying"; "with the timing of the change reflecting how he is thinking" - OCR garbled the next clause).

**CA-018** Thomas and Johnston's "Points to remember when animating emotions": (1) the emotional state is clearly defined; (2) the thought process reveals the feeling, sometimes with a single held drawing or a simple move, …"determine which is best in each case"; (3) use cutting and camera to accentuate; (4) ask "What am I trying to say here? What do I really want to show? How do I want the audience to react?"; (5) use time to establish the emotion, convey it, let viewers savor it, but do not be ponderous.
Source: IL, end of the emotions chapter. Type: primary. Confidence: verified quote (OCR of a numbered list).

**CA-019** Danger zone: scenes of inner struggle tempt the animator to overact and lose credibility; better to let the character reveal feelings in what he does and how, and keep close-ups for emphasis.
Source: IL, emotions chapter ("There is an inherent danger in animating scenes of inner struggle..."). Also cited as p. 482 by the AnimSchool post in CA-049. Type: primary. Confidence: verified quote.

**CA-020** Williams' three-point acting summary: (1) put over the point of the scene clearly; (2) get inside the character (everybody is different); (3) show clearly what they are thinking. Also: "do one thing at a time and be crystal clear"; and what does the character want and, more interesting, why.
Source: SK, "Acting" section (OCR). Type: primary. Confidence: verified quote (OCR).

**CA-021** Pick the one right thing to do and do not let other ideas bury it (Milt Kahl via Williams). One gesture at a time: "we can only put over one thing at a time, just as we can only say one word at a time."
Source: SK, "Acting" and "Attitude" sections (OCR). Type: primary (Williams quoting Kahl). Confidence: verified quote (OCR).

**CA-022** Plan the performance before animating: figure out in advance exactly what you will do, lock down the important poses; Williams cites Bill Tytla working out everything in tiny thumbnails first.
Source: SK, "Acting" section end (OCR). Type: primary. Confidence: paraphrase.

## B. Eyes, blinks, holds

**CA-023** The eyes carry the thought. Thomas and Johnston's expression rules: "Have you the right expression for what your character is thinking? Are all parts of the head and face related to this one idea?" A Disney studio tip repeated by Williams: if short of time, spend it on the eyes; they are what people watch. (A transcription of "Ollie Johnston's 12 Rules for Expressions" on Golan Levin's blog, https://flong.com/archive/blog/2009/johnstons-rules-of-expressions-for-eyes-and-time-travel/index.html, adds "that expression originates in the eyes" and "Eyes in close-up should move 3 frames ahead of accent"; the first line was NOT found in the IL text I read, the second matches IL rule 7 in CA-034. Treat the Levin list as secondary and of unknown origin.)
Source: IL, "Acting and Emotions" chapter, expression rules list (verified in IL text, rules 5-6); SK, "Body Language"/"Eyes" (OCR). Type: primary (IL, SK), secondary (Levin). Confidence: verified quote (IL), paraphrase (SK), single-source (Levin's "originates in the eyes").

**CA-024** Eye darts are not random; they should be super quick, ideally with no in-betweens, and omitting them is a common junior mistake. Too slow reads mushy, too much movement reads floaty, none reads dead.
Source: Animation Apprentice, "How To Animate Eye Darts", https://www.animationapprentice.org/blog/how-to-animate-eye-darts.html (dated 5 Oct 2025 in the search listing). Type: secondary. Confidence: verified quote ("Eye darts should be super quick – ideally with no in-betweens at all.").

**CA-025** Frame count for an eye dart: usually 3 frames (start position, one transitional frame, end position); shifting gaze between points of interest 3-4 frames. Frame rate not stated on the page.
Source: Bloop Animation, "4 Blinks Every Animator Should Know", https://www.bloopanimation.com/blinking-animation/ (no author or date on page). Type: secondary. Confidence: verified quote ("They usually take 3 frames."), single-source for the 3-4 frame range.

**CA-026** The eyes usually move first; the head follows when the thought needs commitment, emphasis or clarity. Offsetting eye and head timing adds believability (head turning while the eyes lag can read as reluctance). "Let gaze lead action. The audience follows where the character looks."
Source: "Lessons: Ten Things to Think About: Eyes", https://www.thinkinganimation.com/eyes (no byline, no date on page). Type: secondary. Confidence: verified quote.

**CA-027** The same page states: "Thought always precedes movement." Eye darts, glances and holds show intent, curiosity, suspicion or hesitation; every eye movement should have a purpose.
Source: same as CA-026. Type: secondary. Confidence: verified quote (single-source).

**CA-028** Our eyes are rarely still; when listening, eyes flick around in a staccato way reflecting the listener's shifting thoughts (Williams' phone-listener example). Use this for the listener in a dialogue shot.
Source: SK, "Body Language"/eyes pages (OCR: "OUR EYES ARE RARELY STILL"; "WHEN LISTENING ON THE PHONE THE EYES FLICKER AROUND IN A STACCATO FASHION REFLECTING THE LISTENER'S SHIFTING THOUGHTS IN REACTION"). Type: primary. Confidence: verified quote (OCR).

**CA-029** A blink marks a change in the thought process: "The most important use of a blink is to show thought process", especially at a change of thought; there is no fixed schedule (the "blink every two seconds" rule is rejected). Rapid blinking can read as shyness, nerves, discomfort or near-tears; withheld blinks as anger, numbness or intensity. Other triggers: wetting the eyes, a rapid head turn, a major change in eye direction.
Source: Animation Mentor newsletter 1105, "Animation Tips & Tricks: Blinks", https://newsletters.animationmentor.com/newsletter/1105/feature_tiptricks.html (author not named on fetch; the Forrest Gump example is given there). Type: secondary. Confidence: verified quote.

**CA-030** Film editor Walter Murch's observation (editing *The Conversation*): the point where he decided to cut was very frequently the point where Gene Hackman blinked. This is an editor's working hypothesis about blinks and thought boundaries, not established science, but it is the origin of "blink = thought change" in animation teaching.
Source: Paris Review conversation with Josh Melnick, quoted at https://candlerblog.com/2012/02/07/murch-on-blinking. Type: primary for the quote (Murch's words), blog host is secondary. Confidence: verified quote ("was the point that Hackman blinked."). The "new whole thought or emotion" wording came only from a search snippet (not verified, see open questions).

**CA-031** Thomas and Johnston on blinks: blinks are good on any shift of eye direction because they call attention to the change; the blink keeps an intense look from going dead and makes a stared-at drawing live again; blinks of disbelief, puzzlement or wonder are usually shown with a slight squint; lids need flesh and thickness; with one in-between, "slow out" of each extreme.
Source: IL, "Eye Blinks" section. Type: primary. Confidence: verified quote (OCR).

**CA-032** Williams: when you turn your head from one thing to another, you will blink en route (unless frightened, then the eyes stay open); adding a blink to a head turn is part of making a plain action interesting.
Source: SK, head-turn and overlap sections (OCR: "The person will blink. The eye, switching focus from one side to the other, will blink en route. (Unless they're frightened - then the eyes …"). Type: primary. Confidence: verified quote (OCR).

**CA-033** Blink timing numbers (24 fps assumed, not stated on the page): regular blink = 2 frames closing, 1 closed, 3 opening; fast blink = 2 closing, 3 opening, no hold; long blink = 3 closing, 2 closed, 4 opening. Blink type by use: regular for stare/head move/boredom; fast for anxiety or anger; long for a long head move or change of thought.
Source: Bloop Animation (CA-025). Type: secondary. Confidence: verified quote of the counts; single-source. Williams' own blink chart is a drawing and did not survive OCR; no Williams blink count is claimed.

**CA-034** Blink used for sync: "the eyes should close ahead of the accent by three to four frames." In an extreme close-up using only eyes for sync, start the eyes into the move at least three frames ahead of the accent; for mushy soft lines 1-2 frames; for strong accents 4-5.
Source: IL, dialogue-sync rules 7 and 8. Type: primary. Confidence: verified quote (OCR).

**CA-035** Holds: a careful pose held without movement "for a few frames - at least eight, maybe as many as sixteen"gives the audience time to absorb the attitude, but a long dead hold flattens the drawing; the answer is the Moving Hold. Thomas and Johnston …"2 feet 8 frames (less than 2 seconds)" is the maximum a drawing can stay on screen without movement, and a blink during the hold recaptures life.
Source: IL, "Moving Hold" passage and "Eye Blinks" section (OCR). Inference to flag: at 16 frames per 35 mm foot, 2 ft 8 frames = 40 frames, about 1.67 s at 24 fps, consistent with "less than 2 seconds"; this conversion is mine. Type: primary. Confidence: verified quote; conversion is an inference.

**CA-036** Moving hold, how to build it: four ingredients - momentum (drift continues the last move's direction, do not start it too early), breathing (small chest/shoulder shifts), eye darts, head support (head follows the body's momentum). No amounts or frame counts are given by the source.
Source: Scott McWhinnie, "Animating Nothing | Create Moving Holds", AnimSchool blog, 2024-11-27, https://blog.animschool.edu/?p=858. Type: secondary (instructor, professional animator). Confidence: verified quote/paraphrase.

**CA-037** Milt Kahl via Williams: "The most difficult thing to do in animation is nothing." Make a plain action interesting by breaking the body into sections and overlapping them (move the foot first, delay the head, throw in a blink) rather than adding gesture.
Source: SK, overlap/"Nothing" pages (OCR). Type: primary. Confidence: verified quote (OCR).

## C. Anticipation, takes, accents, expression timing

**CA-038** Thomas and Johnston: every major action should be preceded by a specific move that anticipates it; "this anticipation can be as small as a change of expression or as big as the broadest physical action." Without it the audience gets restless ("What's he doing?"). A surprise gag only works if a different action was anticipated first. This is the rule for scaling anticipation to intent: the stronger or more important the action, the more visible the preparation; a throwaway beat gets a small look.
Source: IL, "Anticipation" section. Type: primary. Confidence: verified quote (OCR).

**CA-039** Anticipation can be corny when overused; the alternative is a surprise (do something different from what is expected). Williams: "Having too much anticipation can be corny sometimes and crazy takes unnecessary."
Source: SK, "Anticipation" and "Takes and accents" sections (OCR). Type: primary. Confidence: verified quote (OCR).

**CA-040** "A take is an anticipation of an accent which then settles." Williams' worked example: Basil Rathbone's shocked take in a film: head goes up to the accent in 3 frames, then cushions back to a virtual hold; the take is 8 frames long, with the head frozen in the middle of the screen so the take reads.
Source: SK, "Takes and Accents" (OCR: "THE TAKE, THEN, IS 8 FRAMES LONG ... HEAD GOES UP TO THE ACCENT IN 3 FRAMES"). Type: primary. Confidence: verified quote (OCR); numbers are from the OCR of a handwritten chart, check against the printed page. This is the only take frame count from a primary source in this file; no double-take frame count was found.

**CA-041** Invisible anticipation: a one-to-three-frame move in the opposite direction, too fast to see but felt, gives snap to an accent (for example a small dip before looking up at something mildly surprising).
Source: SK, "Invisible Anticipations" (OCR: "ONLY FOR ONE, TWO OR THREE FRAMES"). Type: primary. Confidence: paraphrase, numbers from OCR.

**CA-042** Do not change the expression during a broad move: change it before the move or at the end, when it can be seen. Thomas and Johnston: "Avoid making a fast move while changing the expression"; "Do not lose the expression change in an active secondary action." Williams (Milt Kahl): put the change where you can see it.
Source: IL expression rules 1a-1c; SK "Change of Expression" (OCR). Type: primary. Confidence: verified quote (IL), paraphrase (SK).

**CA-043** Secondary action must support, not compete: wiping a tear, putting on glasses. If it dominates, it is the wrong choice or poorly staged. A change of expression that is the secondary action must come before or after the major move; "a change in the middle of a major move will go unnoticed."
Source: IL, "Secondary Action" section. Type: primary. Confidence: verified quote (OCR).

**CA-044** Gesture order avoids cliche: a bad actor points at the same time as saying the line; point first and then say it (or say it then point), and lead the point with the wrist, hand and fingers last. Williams: this "takes it out of the realm of the cliche."
Source: SK, "Acting Point" (OCR). Type: primary. Confidence: verified quote (OCR).

**CA-045** Contrast makes a change readable: to sell a startle, first give the character a complacent or amused state (reading a book) so the change is big; "look for the contrast" (Milt Kahl via Williams).
Source: SK, "Change of Expression" section (OCR). Type: primary. Confidence: verified quote (OCR).

## D. Acting choices: cliches, specificity, status, reactions, the listener

**CA-046** Cliche, defined two ways: a choice seen too often in similar shots, and the "go-to" choice that comes to mind first (snapping fingers for an idea). Build choices from the character's personality and subtext (the unspoken motive under the line). Study well-regarded live-action films for the choices the actors did NOT make. If a cliche is the right choice, execute it well and pair it with fresh elements. The same post warns that a shot can end up a copy of the animator's own personality.
Source: AnimSchool blog, "Acting choices: Is it a cliche?", 2017-05-22 (URL date), https://blog.animschool.edu/category/acting-choices (no byline on the page; quotes instructors Garrett Shikuma and Mitch Yager). Type: secondary. Confidence: paraphrase of a page I read.

**CA-047** Reacting is acting: do not keep the character moving all the time (a common newcomer mistake); choose the level of performance to fit the moment (a held drawing, a simple move, gestures, or full action); in dialogue shots think about reactions as well as actions.
Source: AnimSchool blog, "Reacting is acting", 2019-05-27 (URL date), same category page. Type: secondary. Confidence: paraphrase.

**CA-048** The listener is half the scene: when the camera is on a character other than the speaker, you often expose the feelings of both at once, one through his lines and the other through his reactions. Thomas and Johnston call this "a good idea to consider at any time."
Source: IL, dialogue staging section ("Camera 'on' a character other than the speaker"). Type: primary. Confidence: verified quote (OCR).

**CA-049** Status as a playable choice. Class notes on Hooks' teaching: high status reads as stillness, direct gaze, ease; low status as looking down, face-touching, tension. Eye contact is selective: intense eye contact is usually intimacy, conflict or threat. The same notes say to build intention before emotion and to keep yelling as a late option because control is often quieter.
Source: https://www.thinkinganimation.com/acting-for-animators (class notes; the page states the wording is the site author's, not a Hooks transcript). Keith Johnstone's *Impro*, the origin of status work, was not read. Type: secondary. Confidence: verified quote of the notes; Hooks' own wording not confirmed.

**CA-050** One important thing on screen at a time; big motions take attention from small motions; everything important must read on the first viewing; give characters time to think: "We see, then judge, then act." Posing checklist: silhouette clear, line of action strong, tilts different, symmetry avoided.
Source: Ferdinand Engländer, "Animation Critique: The quarrel - good acting and boosting clarity", Animator Island, 2015-07-15, https://www.animatorisland.com/?p=3701. Type: secondary. Confidence: verified quote ("Only have one important thing happening on screen at once!"; "We see, then judge, then act!").

**CA-051** Pantomime is the root: Disney art director Ken Anderson (quoted by Williams): pantomime is the basic art of animation; body language is the root and it is universal. Study silent films: much of the acting is hammy but "it's all very clear."
Source: SK, "Body Language" (OCR). Type: primary (Williams quoting Anderson). Confidence: verified quote (OCR).

**CA-052** Body leads the point of the shot: make the point clear with body action first; body attitude echoes facial attitude ("it's all one"); the expression of body and face is more important than the movement. If the body and head are right you can almost get away with the mouths.
Source: SK, "Attitude" (OCR). Type: primary. Confidence: verified quote (OCR).

**CA-053** The acting decisions are made before the animator starts (in boards, rehearsal, shot planning): story artists work out relationships, emotional beats and character business; animators then mostly refine. For an AI director this means the brief must carry the beats, not leave them for the render stage.
Source: Mark Mayerson, "Animators and Acting Part 3", 2006-06-27, https://mayersoncreative.com/articles/animators_and_acting_part3.html. Type: secondary (industry veteran's essay). Confidence: paraphrase of what the article says (it is a workflow essay, not acting rules).

## E. Poses, silhouette, line of action, staging for the camera

**CA-054** Staging, defined by Thomas and Johnston: "the presentation of any idea so that it is completely and unmistakably clear."An action is staged so it is understood, a personality so it is recognizable, an expression so it can be seen, a mood so it …"story point" decides everything else (long shot vs close-up, camera move vs cuts).
Source: IL, "Staging" principle (also quoted by Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twelve_basic_principles_of_animation, p. 53 per its citation). Type: primary (IL text read). Confidence: verified quote.

**CA-055** Staging a single action: only one action is seen; it must not be confused by drapery, a poor angle, or something going on elsewhere; "make the drawings that will stage each idea in the strongest and the simplest way before going on to the next action." Camera distance must suit the idea: a kick is not shown from a waist-up close-up, an expression is not shown in a long shot where the figure is lost.
Source: IL, "Staging". Type: primary. Confidence: verified quote (OCR).

**CA-056** Silhouette: Walt Disney (via IL): "Work in silhouette so that everything can be seen clearly. Don't have a hand come over a face so that you can't see what's happening. …" The authors add that it is always better to show the action in silhouette, and Chaplin held that an actor who knows his emotion thoroughly can show it in silhouette.
Source: IL, "Staging". Type: primary. Confidence: verified quote (OCR). A sentence introducing a "test of the clarity of drawing" is cut by the OCR, so the "fill it in black" test is NOT attributed to IL here.

**CA-057** The twelve principles as Thomas and Johnston list them: Squash and Stretch, Anticipation, Staging, Straight Ahead Action and Pose to Pose, Follow Through and Overlapping Action, Slow In and Slow Out, Arcs, Secondary Action, Timing, Exaggeration, Solid Drawing, Appeal. The ones that matter most for acting direction are Anticipation, Staging, Secondary Action, Timing and Appeal.
Source: IL, list at the opening of the principles chapter. Type: primary. Confidence: verified quote (OCR).

**CA-058** Symmetry/twinning is not forbidden: people delivering a message (preachers, politicians) twin their hands and arms naturally, as an expression of harmony and authority; use it deliberately, and take the curse off by delaying one arm a few frames or tilting another part.
Source: SK, "Symmetry or Twinning" (OCR; the exact frame offset is garbled in the OCR and is NOT stated here). Type: primary. Confidence: paraphrase.

## F. Timing and frame counts (only where a source states a number)

**CA-059** Timing is meaning: two head drawings (leaning right, then left with the chin raised) communicate different ideas depending only on the number of in-betweens: with no in-betweens the character has been hit by a tremendous force; with one in-between, by a brick or rolling pin; further in-betweens read as dodging, a nervous twitch, a crisp order, a friendly "come on". "Neither acting nor attitude could be portrayed without paying very close attention to Timing."
Source: IL, "Timing" principle (OCR; the order of the later in-between meanings is scrambled in the OCR, so only the first two are asserted). Type: primary. Confidence: verified quote for the quoted sentence; paraphrase for the chart.

**CA-060** Ones and twos: "use twos for normal actions and ones for very fast actions; turns always have to be on ones - normal acting on twos." And for rapid dialogue: Brer Fox in *Song of the South* talked so fast that all the dialogue had to be on ones; some words were as short as two exposures and not many were over four.
Source: SK, "The Great Ones and Twos Battle" (OCR); IL, dialogue-sync rule 10. Type: primary. Confidence: verified quote (OCR).

**CA-061** Thomas and Johnston on timing generally: speed of movement defines whether a character reads as lethargic, excited, nervous or relaxed. (Williams: "work level, then advance"; see CA-064.)
Source: IL, "Timing". Type: primary. Confidence: verified quote.

## G. Dialogue acting and lip sync

**CA-062** Ham Luske's sync rules as recorded by Thomas and Johnston, rule 1: anticipate dialogue with head, body or gesture three to four frames ahead of the heavy modulation, but mouth sync should be right on the modulation; a closed-mouth consonant lands on the consonant and, if possible, stays closed for two frames so it is seen; if you work on twos and the mouth falls a frame ahead, that is okay.
Source: IL, "Rules for dialogue" (OCR: "Anticipate dialogue with head, body, or gesture three to four frames ahead of the heavy modulation, but mouth sync should be right on the modulation."). Type: primary. Confidence: verified quote.

**CA-063** The number "mouth leads the sound by one to two frames"is a rule of thumb, not a law. Thomas and Johnston: the animators found that shifting the whole action three frames ahead of the beat …"There seems to be no hard and fast rule on sync". Their advice: experiment by shifting against the track, after honestly animating the feeling of the sound.
Source: IL, sync section (OCR). Type: primary. Confidence: verified quote (OCR).

**CA-064** Williams on picture-to-sound sync: "Do we animate level sync or animate the picture one frame ahead ... or two frames ahead of the sound? Answer: work level." Level (right on the modulation) is the real sync; shifting the picture one, two or three frames ahead is a test result, "never being late". The "2 frames ahead" rule is "a crude rule of thumb", and an editors' habit of demanding two frames ahead is called "a disease" and "tyranny"; run tests at level sync, then advance the picture one to three frames depending on what looks right for this character and voice. For …"Rule of thumb 2": animate the hit 2 frames ahead of the sound, or animate level and advance the picture in the edit (which he prefers).
Source: SK, "Picture and Sound Sync" (OCR). Type: primary. Confidence: verified quote (OCR; the "level" instruction and the "2 frames ... crude rule of thumb" sentences are legible).

**CA-065** A second source stating the same rule of thumb with a larger range: offset the jaw opening "at least two frames before the audio is actually heard" because on the exact frame the sync reads slightly late; for a heavily emphasised vowel or consonant "six to eight frames ahead". Reasoning given: a slight delay between forming a mouth shape and the air carrying the sound.
Source: "Why lipsync should be two frames ahead", Animation Apprentice (page header credits Cliff Nordberg; "posted by" line shows Alexander Williams), https://animationapprentice.blogspot.com/2022/04/why-lipsync-should-be-two-frames-ahead.html (2022-04). Type: secondary. Confidence: verified quote. Fetch truncated at 100,000 characters of 140,959; only the first part was read.

**CA-066** Old masters' practice (Williams): put sharp physical actions and head moves 3 or 4 frames ahead of the modulation, then put the mouth action "on the nose"; "most of the time the head accent is up" (anticipate down, head accent up, then lips on the vowel); there is always an accent unless the speaker is "utterly boring."
Source: SK, "Picture and Sound Sync"/"Head accents" (OCR). Type: primary. Confidence: verified quote (OCR).

**CA-067** Animate the feeling of the word, not the letters: "the key to lip sync is getting the feeling of the word and not the individual letters"; hit only the important vowels and accents; foreign-film dubbing actors match the first vowel and last accent of a sentence and what is between "will work". A common fault: the bottom of the face does not stretch and compress enough, so dialogue looks stiff.
Source: SK, lip sync pages (OCR). Type: primary. Confidence: verified quote (OCR).

**CA-068** Milt Kahl's "secret of lip sync" (via Williams): "progress the action as you speak - go somewhere, anywhere, as you speak."Also: mouth action can be the last thing to work on; Ken Harris learned most about lip sync animating a Martian with no mouth, which …"how many poses for this sentence, for this thought?"; keep it simple.
Source: SK, lip sync pages (OCR). Type: primary. Confidence: verified quote (OCR).

**CA-069** Mouth shapes should carry personality and hold across pauses: look for shapes peculiar to this one voice; give important shapes enough time to be read as "a picture of the word"; on a hold at the end of a line retain a mouth expression that reflects the last word and soften it, rather than snapping to neutral; a stretched-out word is treated like a moving hold (second, stronger extreme and slow into it); few dialogue in-betweens are straight.
Source: IL, dialogue rules 2-6. Type: primary. Confidence: verified quote (OCR).

**CA-070** Listen to your track before acting it: study the phrasing and timing of the line (accents, fast moves, drift moves, pauses) and the personality of the specific delivery; "between them they offer the key to what the character is feeling."
Source: IL, "Listen to Your Track". Type: primary. Confidence: verified quote.

**CA-071** A practical mouth-shape set. Rhubarb Lip Sync documents a nine-shape set: A (closed, P/B/M), B (slightly open, clenched teeth), C (open), D (wide open), E (slightly rounded), F (puckered), and optional G (upper teeth on lower lip, F/V), H (long L, tongue raised) and X (idle/pause). Williams' broader rule: mouths open for vowels A,E,I,O,U and close for consonants B,M,P,F,T,V, with the tongue behind the teeth for N,D,L,TH. Shape count varies by style; simpler styles drop G, H, and L/Th.
Source: Rhubarb Lip Sync README (read as text only, nothing cloned or run), https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DanielSWolf/rhubarb-lip-sync/master/README.adoc; SK lip sync page (OCR). The "simpler styles use five or six shapes" statement was a search-snippet claim, not read in full. Type: secondary (tool documentation) and primary (Williams). Confidence: verified quote (README), single-source for the five or six claim.

## H. Acting on limited, 2D and vector rigs (what survives)

**CA-072** Everything in the book's staging logic was first forced by a rig with almost no articulation: Mickey was solid black with black ears and arms, so a hand in front of the chest vanished and a shrug disappeared; "we learned that it is always better to show the action in silhouette." The lesson for a vector rig: every pose must read as a flat shape, and a gesture that overlaps the face or torso will be lost.
Source: IL, "Staging". Type: primary. Confidence: verified quote (OCR).

**CA-073** Timing alone can carry meaning on a minimal rig (see CA-059): two drawings of a head are enough; the number of in-betweens decides whether it is a hit, a twitch, a dodge, an order or a greeting. This is the cheapest acting lever on a vector rig: head angle plus timing plus holds.
Source: IL, "Timing". Type: primary. Confidence: paraphrase of CA-059 applied to the rig case (the application to vector rigs is my inference).

**CA-074** Hanna-Barbera-era craft (animator Carlo Vinci's early work): a four-position head move instead of the usual two-position head bob; head dropped on twos with stretch and a changing nose angle; hold the mouth for a frame while the eyes close or look elsewhere, which "gives the impression of extra movement"; keep the head stationary while the mouth moves around the face.
Source: Yowp (blog), "Getting the most out of limited animation", 2010-03-15, https://yowpyowp.blogspot.com/2010/03/getting-most-out-of-limited-animation.html. Type: secondary (animation-history blog; analyses specific scenes). Confidence: verified quote ("gives the impression of extra movement"); no drawing counts given.

**CA-075** Limited-animation principles from UPA and Zagreb practitioners: economy of gesture (Jules Engel on Bobe Cannon: "There isn't a gesture on the stage that is not truly necessary"); movement "based on feelings, rather than anatomy" (Pete Docter on UPA); uneven timing (a crossing-signal arm moving up and down at different speeds); and "How smooth the animation looks has nothing to do with how many frames there are" (an animator described as from Toei's early days). Holds of one to three frames create a stepped look (unnamed supervisor on *Spider-Man: Into the Spider-Verse*).
Source: Animation Obsessive, "Don't Call It 'Limited' Animation", https://animationobsessive.substack.com/p/dont-call-it-limited-animation (reporting; quotes attributed on the page). Type: secondary. Confidence: verified quote; attributions are the article's.

**CA-076** A head-only performance still has a hierarchy: Williams' Ken Harris example shows that when the mouth is absent the head accent has to do all the work, so on a limited rig put the accents on head tilt and eye direction first and treat the mouth as the last layer.
Source: SK, lip sync pages (OCR). Type: primary. Confidence: paraphrase of CA-068; the application to limited rigs is my inference.

# Rules for the skill

Imperative rules the skill can apply when writing a shot brief or reviewing a render. Each cites the entries it rests on.

## R-A: Writing the shot brief

1. **Give every shot an objective, an action (tactic) and an obstacle**, written as a provable "doing", never as a mood. Template: "The character wants X (objective); plays Y to get it (action); Z is in the way (obstacle)." (CA-001, CA-002, CA-003, CA-010)
2. **Do not brief an emotion.** Brief the intention and let the emotion be the result of the obstacle; list the emotion only as an outcome. (CA-004, CA-049)
3. **Write where the shot starts and what comes before and after** (scenes begin in the middle). (CA-008)
4. **Split the shot into beats**, each a change of tactic or a new thought, and give each beat its own key expression or pose. (CA-006, CA-017, CA-018)
5. **Name the thought before each action** and put a thinking beat (eye move, brow change, short hold) in front of it. (CA-009, CA-014, CA-015, CA-016)
6. **Always include the listener** in a dialogue shot, with her own objective and reactions, and say when the camera cuts to her. (CA-047, CA-048, CA-028)
7. **Pick a status relationship** between characters and say which signals carry it (stillness, gaze, posture); treat as a choice, not a rule. (CA-049) (secondary source)
8. **Brief one important idea per moment**; say which element has priority when two things move. (CA-021, CA-050, CA-055)
9. **Ban the go-to gesture**: for each beat, name the obvious choice and one alternative drawn from the character's personality or subtext; keep the cliche only if it is the clearest and add something fresh. (CA-044, CA-046, CA-012)
10. **Decide the performance level** per beat (a held pose, a simple move, a gesture, full action). Do not default to constant motion. (CA-018, CA-047)
11. **Stage for the camera before choosing the pose**: story point first, then camera distance that shows the thing (expression = close, kick = full body). (CA-054, CA-055)

## R-B: Eyes, blinks, holds

12. **Put the thought in the eyes.** Specify eye direction per beat; eyes lead the head by a beat when a decision is being made. (CA-023, CA-026, CA-027)
13. **Write eye darts only with a reason** (looking for, at, or thinking); keep them very fast, with no in-betweens; about 3 frames is a safe starting point (single source). (CA-024, CA-025)
14. **Blink on thought changes, head turns and major eye-direction changes, not on a timer**; use a fast blink for anxiety, a long blink for a long head move or change of thought; start from 2 frames close / 1 closed / 3 open (24 fps assumed) and adjust. (CA-029, CA-031, CA-032, CA-033)
15. **Never freeze a held pose.** Anything that holds longer than about 8-16 frames needs a moving hold (breath, momentum drift, eye dart, a blink); the book's ceiling is under 2 seconds with no movement. (CA-035, CA-036, CA-037)

## R-C: Anticipation, takes, expression timing

16. **Scale anticipation to the importance of the action**, from a change of expression to a full wind-up. Use a 1-3 frame invisible anticipation to add snap. (CA-038, CA-041)
17. **For a take**, preload an anticipation or a held neutral, then a quick accent (about 3 frames to the accent in Williams' 8-frame example) that settles; do not over-anticipate. (CA-040, CA-039)
18. **Never change the expression during a fast move.** Put the change before the move or after it, where it can be seen. (CA-042, CA-043)
19. **Build contrast before a change** (a calm or complacent state before a startle). (CA-045)
20. **Order the gesture and the line**: point first then say it, or the reverse; do not do both together. (CA-044)

## R-D: Dialogue and lip sync

21. **Animate the feeling of the line, not the letters.** Hit the important vowels and consonants only; keep one key expression per thought. (CA-017, CA-067, CA-070)
22. **Lead with head, body or gesture 3-4 frames ahead of the heavy accent; mouth on the modulation.** (CA-062, CA-066)
23. **Treat "mouth leads sound by 1-2 frames" as a starting point, not a law.** Test at level sync, then shift the picture 1-3 frames earlier and judge; some shots need more (up to 8 for a short emphatic word in one Disney scene; 6-8 for emphasised vowels in one blog). (CA-063, CA-064, CA-065) This is a source disagreement; see Open questions.
24. **Hold closed-mouth consonants (M, B, P) about 2 frames** so they are seen. (CA-062)
25. **Eyes-only sync in an extreme close-up**: start the eyes at least 3 frames before the accent; a blink to help sync closes 3-4 frames before the accent. (CA-034)
26. **Use the mouth shape set that fits the style** (nine shapes, or fewer for simple styles) and let each shape reflect the voice's personality; keep a softened mouth shape across pauses. (CA-069, CA-071)
27. **Put the character's body in progress while speaking**: "go somewhere, anywhere, as you speak." (CA-068)

## R-E: Simple, 2D and vector rigs

28. **Every pose must read as a flat silhouette**; keep hands off the face and torso. (CA-056, CA-072)
29. **On a minimal rig, put the performance in head angle, eye direction, timing and holds** (two drawings plus the right number of in-betweens is enough to change meaning). (CA-059, CA-073, CA-074)
30. **Use holds and stepped timing deliberately** (1-3 frame holds, hold the mouth while the eyes move) rather than animating every frame. (CA-074, CA-075)
31. **If the mouth is absent or minimal, the head accent does the lip-sync work**; do mouth last. (CA-068, CA-076)

## R-F: Review checklist (playblast)

32. **Freeze-frame test** (CA-003), **thinking-beat test** (CA-014, CA-016), **silhouette test** (CA-056), **one-idea test** (CA-050), **expression-change-placement test** (CA-042), **hold test** (CA-035), **sync test by shifting the track 1-3 frames** (CA-063, CA-064), **cliche test** (CA-046), and **listener test** (CA-048). Use CA-077 and CA-078 as the report template.
33. **Weak acting signals**: constant motion, emotion without objective, dead eyes, hidden expression change, missing or corny anticipation, unreadable silhouette, simultaneous actions. (CA-078)
