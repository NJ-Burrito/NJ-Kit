Reel 1 closes on the words *Make No Mistakes*; reel 2 has no closing card and
simply ends on the full list under a countdown timer; reel 3 cuts at 40.2 s to
a call-to-action that is not part of its list; reel 4 simply ends.

**The four numbering schemes are kept apart on purpose.** Bare numbers (1–19)
always mean reel 1. Reel 2's twenty are prefixed **H**, reel 3's ten **L**, reel
4's twenty **S** — so that a report row can never be traced to the wrong source.

**Four lists, and after four reels they still do not share a single item.**
That is worth naming, because it is the reason this skill keeps growing rather
than converging: each reel asks a different question of the same site. Reel 1
asks whether it is *lawful*, reel 2 whether it is *finished*, reel 3 whether it
is *honest over time*, reel 4 whether it is *safe*. The four questions have
different answerers, and the report keeps them in separate sections for exactly
that reason.

Treat the provenance honestly: **these are market signals, not
specifications.** They cite no standard, they are one creator's lists of
worries, and several items on each are things no browser can determine. What
makes them worth a skill is that the worries are the right ones and the
ordering is a real risk ordering. Every point below has been mapped to the
standard it actually touches — or explicitly marked *convention, not a
standard*, which most of Group D is — and the ones that map to no checkable
property are marked as such rather than quietly dropped.

**One lesson from reading reel 2, kept because it will recur.** A web fetch of
the reel's page returned seventeen of its twenty items, in the wrong order,
with three of the reel's own ✗ marks silently dropped and one item reported as
unreadable. Read from the frames. A list that arrives already-transcribed is a
draft, not a source.
